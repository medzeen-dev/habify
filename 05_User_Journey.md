# Purpose

This document describes the participant journey through habify30.

Rather than documenting the technical process, it explains the intended psychological journey.

Every interaction is designed to answer one question:

> "What does the participant need at this exact moment to maximise the probability of behavioural change?"

The journey therefore follows changes in motivation, confidence, identity and behaviour—not software screens.

---

# Design Philosophy

Most digital learning journeys optimise content consumption.

habify30 optimises behavioural implementation.

This changes almost every design decision.

Participants should never feel like they are completing an online course.

Instead, they should experience habify30 as a lightweight companion that repeatedly brings their attention back to one specific behaviour until it becomes part of everyday work.

The platform should support work—not become additional work.

---

# The Emotional Journey

The participant does not experience a linear process.

Instead, they typically move through predictable psychological states.

Inspiration

↓

Optimism

↓

Reality

↓

Resistance

↓

Experimentation

↓

Small Successes

↓

Confidence

↓

Routine

↓

Habit

habify30 is designed around these transitions.

---

# Stage 1 — Motivation

## Typical Situation

The participant has just completed a workshop or development programme.

Motivation is usually high.

Intentions are genuine.

Confidence is often unrealistically optimistic.

Typical thoughts include:

- "This makes total sense."
- "I'll definitely do this."
- "This time will be different."

Paradoxically, this is not the moment where behavioural support is most needed.

People already want to change.

---

## Design Objective

Capture motivation before everyday work reduces attention.

The participant should explicitly choose one behavioural focus.

The emphasis is not on ambition.

The emphasis is on clarity.

---

# Stage 2 — Commitment

The first important decision is behavioural selection.

One of the central assumptions of habify30 is:

People change behaviour more reliably when they focus on one meaningful behaviour rather than many.

Behaviour should therefore be:

Specific.

Observable.

Relevant.

Within the participant's control.

The participant makes an explicit commitment to this behaviour.

This commitment marks the transition from learning to implementation.

---

# Stage 3 — First Contact with Reality

Usually within the first days, everyday work returns.

Meetings.

Pressure.

Interruptions.

Existing routines.

This is where many traditional learning interventions effectively end.

habify30 intentionally begins here.

---

## Psychological Shift

The participant discovers:

Changing behaviour is harder than expected.

This is not considered failure.

It is considered normal.

One of the product's responsibilities is therefore to normalise difficulty.

Participants should never interpret early inconsistency as evidence that they cannot change.

---

# Stage 4 — Behavioural Experiments

Rather than demanding permanent change, habify30 invites experimentation.

This distinction is deliberate.

Changing identity feels difficult.

Running an experiment feels manageable.

Participants repeatedly ask themselves:

"What happens if I try this today?"

instead of

"I must always do this."

This creates psychological safety while still increasing behavioural repetition.

---

# Stage 5 — Reflection

Reflection is intentionally frequent.

But it is intentionally lightweight.

The objective is not writing diaries.

The objective is increasing awareness.

Typical reflection focuses on:

What happened?

What triggered the behaviour?

What prevented it?

What surprised me?

What will I try next?

Reflection always points towards future behaviour.

Never towards self-judgement.

---

# Stage 6 — Peer Support

Behaviour change often becomes fragile when it remains invisible.

Peer interaction serves three purposes.

## Visibility

Commitments become real when another person knows about them.

---

## Perspective

Peers notice patterns participants overlook themselves.

---

## Encouragement

Behaviour change rarely develops in a straight line.

Small setbacks should become normal conversation rather than private disappointment.

---

# Why Small Peer Groups?

One important design decision was the use of small peer groups.

Large communities encourage passive participation.

Small groups increase psychological safety.

Participants are more likely to contribute.

Relationships develop more naturally.

Individual commitments become visible.

Accountability becomes personal without becoming controlling.

---

# Stage 7 — Momentum

After approximately two weeks, motivation usually plays a smaller role.

Behaviour increasingly depends on routines.

habify30 gradually shifts attention away from inspiration towards consistency.

Questions become simpler.

Did you do it?

What helped?

What got in the way?

What will you try tomorrow?

The platform intentionally reduces complexity over time.

---

# Stage 8 — Completion

The programme formally ends.

Behavioural development does not.

Completion should not communicate:

"You are finished."

Instead it should communicate:

"You have built something you can now continue without us."

The platform should gradually become unnecessary.

This is considered success.

---

# Identity Shift

One implicit objective of the journey is identity development.

Repeated behaviour slowly changes self-perception.

Instead of thinking:

"I'm trying to become someone who gives better feedback."

Participants gradually think:

"I am someone who gives good feedback."

habify30 does not explicitly teach identity theory.

But many design decisions intentionally support this transition.

---

# Product Success

The journey succeeds when participants no longer need reminders.

The desired behaviour becomes part of everyday work.

At this point habify30 has fulfilled its purpose.

Ironically, the ideal outcome is that participants think less and less about the platform itself.

---

# Design Decisions

Several important decisions shape the user journey.

| Decision | Rationale |
|-----------|-----------|
| One behaviour at a time | Reduces cognitive load and increases implementation probability |
| Small experiments | Lowers psychological resistance |
| Lightweight reflection | Fits everyday work |
| Peer accountability | Increases follow-through without external pressure |
| Daily integration | Behaviour should occur during work rather than outside it |
| Gradual reduction of support | Encourages independence rather than dependency |

---

# Open Questions

Several aspects still require further design work.

- How often should reminders be delivered?
- Which reflection formats create the highest behavioural impact?
- How should peer interaction be facilitated?
- How can completion best support long-term maintenance without extending programme duration unnecessarily?
